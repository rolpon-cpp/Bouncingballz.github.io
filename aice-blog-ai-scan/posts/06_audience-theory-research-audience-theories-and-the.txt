An audience is a group of people who are interested in or consume a specific type of content from a certain genre or person. Media producers need to understand their audiences in order to design successful media and appeal to them. Media companies like PBS Kids or NBC understand their audiences and design their production to adapt. PBS Kids’ audience includes children, so they invest in children’s television. NBC’s audience includes adults looking to be informed, so they display news content.

The Uses and Gratifications Theory suggests that audiences actively play a role in choosing what media they consume. If a consumer dislikes something, they will pick an alternative. People watch media for different reasons; they may consume something because it’s part of their identity, it’s entertaining, to be informed, to complete daily tasks, or to better connect with peers. A good example of this is Google Chrome. Chrome is a web browsing tool to complete common tasks online. It satisfied people because it allowed them to browse websites quickly. Media producers can learn that in order for their products to succeed. They need to either empower identities, entertain, inform, or provide to their audience.

Gauntlett’s Identity Theory states that audiences use media to help form their identities, and that identity is something that constantly changes, shaped by numerous factors. Audiences watch media that they enjoy to learn from; they begin to learn from role models and personality traits from which they experiment with. Representation is important since it allows more people to learn about personalities, causing variety in people. An example of a media group that appeals to an audience is K-Pop. K-Pop appeals to a global audience, with Koreans as a focus. It appeals to a lot of people by providing catchy pop music. Producers can use this theory to include a variety of perspectives in their content, causing people to relate to the media and thus increasing success.

Hall’s Reception Theory states that a media producer will embed their values in a piece of media, but allow the audience to interpret it based off their own beliefs and ideas. Preferred reading is when the audience interprets just how the original author intended. Negotiated reading is when the audience interprets the main core message but interprets other parts of the media differently because of their own world views. Oppositional reading is when the audience goes against the author’s original meaning and interprets it the opposite way.

A good example of this is the book “Animal Farm” by George Orwell. A commonly contested detail in the book is the fact that the pigs simplify the rules of the farm to a mantra: “two legs good, four legs bad” in order to teach the dumber animals the core ideas. Some believe that this is dangerous because it aggressively simplifies the rights and rules; others could believe it is justified because there is no practical way to communicate to dumber animals the rights they have. Audiences will interpret this as well as other details differently due to how detailed yet vague the book is.

I think Gauntlett’s Identity Theory makes the most sense. People (including me) have their personality formed based off the media we consume. Especially due to hyper-optimized algorithms in social media now, the media we receive is designed to feed into our own personality and preferences first beyond anything else. These theories help media producers understand their audiences and how exactly their media will be consumed, allowing them to optimize for maximum success and growth. I believe the Uses and Gratifications Theory will be most useful for me though, simply because trying to develop media that could be interpreted differently is difficult to do in something like a magazine versus a book.
