SCENE 2: THE JOB NOBODY APPLIED FOR (0:45-2:00)
VISUAL: Zoom out from Tyler's name to an animated calendar with
shield, clock and bell icons.
NARRATOR: First, a fact that gets lost in the jokes. Most Discord
moderators are volunteers. No salary, no benefits, and usually no
training. A server owner picks someone active, hands them a role,
and says "keep it clean."
VISUAL: A mod's "shift" timeline with notification bubbles: 7:00 AM

gift links. 4:45 PM two friends fighting in voice chat. 3:12 AM a
stranger floods the server with offensive messages.
NARRATOR: Spam, scams, fights, trolls, and the occasional person who
is just having a rough day. All of it handled between school, work
and sleep.
VISUAL: Phone buzzes on a dark bedroom desk; a tired hand reaches
for it (illustrated).
NARRATOR: So why does the internet picture a power-hungry tyrant
instead? It comes down to four reasons.
TEXT: Four face-down cards numbered 1 to 4 fan out.

SCENE 3: WHERE THE STEREOTYPE COMES FROM (2:00-4:15)
Reason 1, The tiny crown.
VISUAL: Card 1 flips. Crown drops onto Tyler's name. A "Ban Member"
button swells to fill the screen, glowing.
NARRATOR: A mod role comes with real buttons: mute, kick, ban.
Online, there is almost no check on how they get used. No manager,
no handbook, no appeals form. Give anyone that button and some will
press it too fast.
Reason 2, You only see the punishments.
VISUAL: Card 2 flips. Split screen. Left "What members see": one red
BANNED message. Right "What mods do": dozens of tiny green checks
(deleted spam, blocked scam link, calmed argument).
TEXT (corner): "Illustrative, not real data"
NARRATOR: When a mod quietly stops a scam, nobody notices. When a
mod bans someone, a whole channel watches. Good moderation looks
like nothing happening. Bad moderation makes a screenshot.
Reason 3, How mods get picked.
VISUAL: Card 3 flips. Tyler's profile: "Member since: 3 weeks ago".
A cartoon friend group behind a velvet rope.
NARRATOR: In many servers the new mod is whoever is online the most,
or whoever the owner already knows. Plenty of them are great.
Others are learning how to handle authority in real time, in
public, with an audience.
Reason 4, Memes turn stories into a type.
VISUAL: Card 4 flips. Fast montage of ORIGINAL meme-style images: a
cartoon mod swinging a giant hammer, a "touch grass" caption, a
parody "mod abuse" screenshot.
NARRATOR: The internet's favorite hobby is turning a few bad stories
into a character. A handful of screenshots become a joke, the joke
becomes a meme, and the meme becomes a type. After that, every
strict mod is proof, and every kind one is an exception.

SCENE 4: WHY IT KEEPS HAPPENING (4:15-5:45)
VISUAL: A loop diagram draws itself: "No training" > "Overreacts to
trouble" > "Members feel powerless" > "Posts the story" >
"Stereotype grows" > "Patient mods quit" > back to "No training".
NARRATOR: Here is the uncomfortable part. The stereotype is not just
a joke. It feeds itself.
Beat 1, Unpaid jobs pay in status.
VISUAL: "Salary" bar stays empty; "Respect" bar fills slowly.
NARRATOR: When the only reward is respect, the role becomes part of
a person's identity. A challenge to the rules starts to feel like a
challenge to them.
Beat 2, Mods see the worst of the internet daily.
VISUAL: Replay the opening skit with a thought bubble over Tyler:
"That is the 40th 'when is the update' today." Jess's question gets
a red "SPAM?" label.
NARRATOR: After the hundredth troll, everyone starts to look like
the next one. Jess was not a troll. Tyler just stopped being able
to tell.
Beat 3, There is no appeal.
VISUAL: Jess's empty chat box. A "Contact the moderators" button
leads to a locked door.
NARRATOR: If a ban feels unfair and there is no way to challenge
it, the banned person has one outlet left: complain loudly
somewhere else. That is where most of the stories you have seen
came from.
Beat 4, The wrong people stay.
VISUAL: A line of mod avatars. Calm ones fade out with "has left the
server" messages. The one holding the hammer stays.
NARRATOR: Burnout drives out the patient mods first. The ones who
enjoy control stay the longest. So the stereotype slowly becomes
truer, even though it started as unfair.

VISUAL: Morning. Members chat cheerfully in a spotless #general.

NARRATOR: Meet Priya. She is the reason your server had a calm
morning. No thank-you, no screenshot, no applause. Just a clean

NARRATOR: The best moderation is invisible, which is exactly why the
bad moderation is what everyone remembers.
VISUAL: Three cards slide in with checklists.
If you are a member: read the rules before posting; if banned,

If you are a mod: warn before you ban and say why; keep a written
log of actions; take breaks and share the load.
If you run a server: write clear rules; build a real appeal process;
thank your team out loud.
